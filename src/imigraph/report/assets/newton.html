How the bouncing ball works

The ball is a rigid object made of several walker nodes: every lattice point with i² + j² ≤ r² around its centre, each one a walker attached to a site of the room. All walkers share one velocity v = (vx, vy) in cells per step. One step moves every walker |vx| edges along the sign of vx and |vy| edges along the sign of vy, the hops interleaved so that the lattice path hugs the straight line (the order a line-drawing algorithm uses). Between the walls this is free Newtonian motion: constant velocity, a straight line, no force, no friction.

The walls are the self-loop edges {s,s} of the boundary sites, as everywhere in imigraph. A hop in a direction in which any walker of the ball faces a self-loop is a bounce: that velocity component flips sign and the hop is taken the other way, and the remaining hops of the step continue with the flipped velocity. That is a specular, totally elastic reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, the speed is unchanged, the ball keeps its shape (it is rigid: all walkers flip together, so it never folds through itself). Compared with the hard-disk gas of the gas report this is the single-body limit: one object, walls only, so the energy and the magnitude of the momentum are exact invariants rather than statistical ones. Compared with world 1 the difference is the walker: a Brownian walker draws a new direction every step and has no momentum to conserve; a lattice-gas walker keeps a unit heading; the ball's walkers carry an integer velocity vector and move as one body. Gravity, several balls colliding, and non-integer velocities are the natural next steps.
